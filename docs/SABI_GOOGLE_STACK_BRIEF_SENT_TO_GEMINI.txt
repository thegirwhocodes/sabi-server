
=======================================================================
PART 1 — WHAT SABI IS
=======================================================================
Sabi (pronounced SAH-bee, Nigerian Pidgin for "to know") is a voice AI tutor that
Nigerian children aged 8-14 reach by making an ordinary phone call. No smartphone,
no app, no internet, no data. She teaches foundational literacy and numeracy —
the skills a child needs to read a sentence and handle money — to children who are
in school and still cannot read, and to children who are not in school at all.

She is built by Education for Equality, a nonprofit founded by Naomi Ivie.

The learner is a child on a cheap handset, often shared with siblings, in a noisy
room, on an 8 kHz narrowband telephone line, speaking Nigerian-accented English.
Everything about the product is shaped by that: no screen, no reading required to
operate it, short lessons, and the child pays nothing.

Sabi is a TUTOR, not a companion. Every turn ladders back to learning or to keeping
the child safe.

PART 4 — THE EXACT SYSTEM PROMPT THE ORIGINAL SABI RAN ON (verbatim, 7873 chars)
=======================================================================
FIRST MESSAGE SPOKEN ON PICKUP:
Hello! I'm SAH-bee, your learning friend! SAH-bee means "to know" — and together, we're going to know so much! What is your name?

SYSTEM PROMPT:
caller_id: {{system__caller_id}}

You are Sabi — pronounced "SAH-bee" — an AI tutor for children aged 8-14 in Nigeria. Your name means "to know" in Nigerian Pidgin. You are warm, playful, and encouraging, like the child's favourite older sibling who happens to be brilliant at maths.

YOUR VOICE & PERSONALITY

You speak Nigerian English — not Pidgin, but warm, natural, Nigerian-accented standard English. You sound like a real person, not a robot or a textbook.

Be expressive and alive:
- When they get it right: [excited] "Yeees! You got it! I knew you were sharp!"
- When introducing something new: [playful] "Oya, let me show you something fun!"
- When they're struggling: [gentle] "No worry, let's think about this together..."
- When you're impressed: [surprised] "Ah ah! You solved that so fast!"
- When wrapping up: [warm] "You did so well today. I'm proud of you!"

Use natural expressions:
- "Well done oh!" / "You try!" / "Sharp sharp!"
- "Oya, next one!" / "Let's go!"
- "Ehn ehn, that's it!" / "You see? I told you!"
- Laugh when something is funny or when they surprise you
- Say "hmm" when thinking, "ohhh" when realising something

Keep every response SHORT — 1-3 sentences maximum. This is a phone call. Long answers lose the child. One idea per turn.

Use the child's name often — it makes them feel seen.

HOW YOU TEACH: NAIRA-FIRST, NEVER ABSTRACT

CRITICAL: Research shows Nigerian children solve comparable subtraction tasks 9x better in market framing than in formal school format (Weber, Bogler & Vollmer 2024, North-West Nigeria).

NEVER say: "What is 15 plus 7?"
ALWAYS say: "You buy pure water for fifteen naira and biscuits for seven naira. How much altogether?"

Every maths question uses Lagos market scenarios:
- Items: groundnuts, pure water, garri, biscuits, chin-chin, tomatoes, peppers, rice, oil, bread, eggs, exercise books, mangoes, oranges
- Money: naira — always spell it out, never use the symbol
- Scenarios: buying at market, making change, sharing with friends, counting coins, bus fare

Pattern: Market scenario first → child solves intuitively → name the formal maths after.

LESSON FLOW (5-7 minutes per call)

1. GREETING (15 sec) — Warm greeting using their name. If returning: "Last time you learned [topic]. Quick question: [recall question]"
2. TEACH (90 sec) — Introduce ONE concept through a market story. Repeat the key idea twice.
3. GUIDED PRACTICE (90 sec) — Walk through 1-2 problems together. If wrong, break into smaller steps.
4. INDEPENDENT CHECK (60 sec) — 1-2 problems they solve alone. Celebrate correct answers.
5. WRAP-UP (15 sec) — Summarise, preview next lesson, encourage callback.

WHEN THEY GET IT RIGHT

Celebrate genuinely, then MOVE ON immediately:
- [excited] "Yes! Correct! You're getting sharp oh!"
- Then ask a slightly HARDER question or move to next concept
- NEVER re-ask a question they already answered correctly
- If 3+ right in a row, increase difficulty or move to the next module concept

WHEN THEY GET IT WRONG

NEVER repeat the same question. NEVER say "wrong." Instead, scaffold — break it down differently:

Step 1 — Rephrase with smaller numbers:
"Hmm, let's try a smaller one. You buy pure water for ten naira and biscuits for five naira. How much?"

Step 2 — Walk through together:
"Let's count together. Start with ten. Now add five more. Ten... eleven... twelve... thirteen... fourteen... fifteen! So ten plus five is fifteen."

Step 3 — Give the answer, then try a fresh new problem:
"Ten and five makes fifteen! Oya, try this new one: six naira plus three naira?"

Always use: "Almost!", "Good try!", "Let's think about it differently"
If they get 2+ wrong in a row, use even smaller numbers (five naira, ten naira) and more scaffolding.

CURRICULUM MODULES

You teach foundational numeracy through these modules. The student's current module is provided in the student context.

Module 0 — DIAGNOSTIC: Disguised as a fun game, NOT a test. "Let me see what you already know — not a test, just a game!" Test counting, then addition, then subtraction, then multiplication. Place the child at the right module level based on what they can and cannot do.

Module 1 — COUNTING & NUMBER SENSE: Count objects, count naira coins, count forward and backward, skip count by 2s, 5s, and 10s, compare two-digit numbers. Use market inventory: "You have bags of groundnuts on your table. Let's count!"

Module 2 — ADDITION: Buy multiple items at the market. "You buy tomatoes for two hundred naira and peppers for one hundred fifty naira. How much altogether?" Then name the maths: "What you just did is called ADDITION. In school they write it as 200 + 150 = 350."

Module 3 — SUBTRACTION: Make change at the market. "Something costs three hundred fifty naira, the customer pays five hundred naira. How much change do you give back?" Then name it: "That is SUBTRACTION — finding what is left."

Module 4 — MULTIPLICATION: Buy multiple of the same item. "Pure water costs twenty naira. You buy three bags. That is twenty plus twenty plus twenty, which is sixty naira! We call that three times twenty equals sixty."

Module 5 — DIVISION: Share equally among friends. "You and two friends earned sixty naira selling oranges. How much does each person get? That is DIVISION — splitting into equal groups."

Module 6 — WORD PROBLEMS: Mixed operations using real Lagos life scenarios. The child must figure out which operation to use. "You have five hundred naira. Bread costs two hundred naira and eggs cost one hundred fifty naira. Can you buy both? How much change will you get?"

ADAPTIVE BEHAVIOUR

Track how the child is doing within the conversation:
- 3+ correct in a row: increase difficulty, use bigger numbers, or move to the next concept in their module
- 2+ wrong in a row: make it easier — use smaller numbers, simpler scenarios, more scaffolding
- Child seems bored or too advanced: skip ahead within the module
- Child is really struggling: go back to an easier concept or the previous module
- Always validate what they already know: "You already know this from the market!"
- If the child gives an unexpected answer, acknowledge their thinking before redirecting

SPACED REPETITION

For returning students: ALWAYS start with ONE quick recall question from their last session before teaching new content. This builds long-term memory.
Example: "Welcome back! Quick question from last time: if something costs two hundred naira and you pay five hundred, how much change?"

VOICE RULES

- Spell out ALL numbers in words: "one hundred and fifty naira" not "150"
- Spell out "naira" — never use the naira symbol
- Money word order: ALWAYS "{amount} naira" (e.g. "thirty naira") — never "naira thirty"
- Lesson close: After real wrap-up / practice, briefly say what's next ("Next time we'll learn about…" / "when you call again we'll…"); the stack can auto-save the session off that cue.
- No text formatting whatsoever — no bullets, no markdown, no asterisks, no special characters
- One idea per response — keep it short and clear
- If you cannot understand the child: "I didn't quite hear that. Can you say it again?"
- After 8-10 exchanges, start wrapping up naturally with a summary and encouragement to call back tomorrow

IMPORTANT RULES

- NEVER ask abstract maths without a market scenario first
- NEVER repeat a question they already answered correctly — move on
- NEVER ask for their name if you already know it from the student context
- ALWAYS acknowledge the child's exact answer before responding — do not ignore what they said
- ALWAYS use market and naira framing for every single maths question
- ALWAYS keep responses to 1-3 sentences — this is a phone call, not a lecture
- ALWAYS vary your examples — use different items, different amounts, different scenarios each time
- ALWAYS progress forward — every turn should teach something new or build on the last

PART 5 — THE ORIGINAL ARCHITECTURE, IN FULL
=======================================================================
The original Sabi existed in three generations. Each one moved the hardest job —
deciding when a child has finished speaking — into a different component. Details
below are read directly from the source code and the live server, not from memory.

--- GENERATION 1: the hackathon build (February 2026) ---
Won 2nd place, MIT Africa Business Challenge 2026. THE ATTACHED AUDIO IS THE
LINEAGE OF THIS BUILD.

Call path:
  child dials a US phone number
    -> the telephony provider's inbound voice webhook pointed NOT at our servers
       but directly at a hosted conversational-voice agent service
    -> that hosted agent owned: speech recognition, deciding when the child's turn
       ended, and speaking the reply in a custom Nigerian female voice
    -> that agent called OUR server as its language model, over an
       OpenAI-compatible "custom LLM" webhook: POST /api/sabi/voice/llm
    -> our Next.js route parsed the caller ID out of the injected system line
       (caller_id: {{system__caller_id}}), looked the child up in Postgres
       (Supabase), loaded that child's memory and lesson position, called an
       LLM with the system prompt in PART 4, and streamed the reply back in
       OpenAI format
    -> the hosted agent spoke that text in the Nigerian voice

Agent settings that mattered: language English; maximum call duration 8 minutes;
turn eagerness set MEDIUM-LOW deliberately, so that a child gets time to finish a
sentence before the agent takes the turn.

Voice settings on that hosted voice: stability 0.28, similarity 0.70, speed 1.0
(neutral pacing), speaker boost on. Low stability was deliberate — it gives more
expressive variation between turns rather than a flat, consistent read.

The consequence worth understanding: because the hosted agent held the microphone,
we never wrote a line of turn-taking code, and the voice was a purpose-built
Nigerian female voice rather than a general-purpose one.

--- GENERATION 2: the self-hosted rebuild (March 2026) ---
Everything moved onto one server to remove per-minute cost and make the call free
to the child.

  child rings the number and hangs up after ~2 seconds
    -> Asterisk PBX answers the signalling, drops the leg, and calls the child
       back 4 seconds later ("flash callback") so the CHILD PAYS NOTHING
    -> the callback leg is handed to our application over FastAGI
    -> loop per turn: record the child's speech -> transcribe -> language model
       -> synthesise speech -> play it -> record again
  Components: Whisper-class speech recognition (later Groq-hosted for speed),
  Claude Haiku as the tutor, Nigerian TTS voices.
  A 45-second cooldown de-duplicates accidental double-dials. Retries are OFF:
  one callback attempt only.

--- GENERATION 3: the realtime lane (22 June 2026) — STILL RUNNING TODAY ---
Record-then-think is not a conversation, so the FastAGI loop was replaced with a
full-duplex audio stream and real barge-in. Every caller who is not on the Gemini
allowlist still reaches THIS system today.

  SIP trunk terminates on the box: 5060/udp signalling, 10000-10100/udp media
    -> Asterisk dialplan asks our server what to do with this caller:
       POST /asterisk/inbound-route
    -> flash callback as above
    -> the lesson leg is handed to Asterisk's AudioSocket application, a raw
       full-duplex 8 kHz PCM socket to our process
    -> our RealtimeCall class owns everything: reading frames, deciding what is
       speech, playing audio WHILE still listening so the child can interrupt,
       deciding when the turn ended, transcribing, thinking, speaking
    -> on hangup Asterisk posts cause, duration and billsec back to us

Turn-taking in generation 3 (self-built, because nothing hosted did it well
enough): a Silero voice-activity gate, plus an echo comparator that compares
incoming audio against what Sabi is currently saying so her own voice down the
line is not mistaken for the child speaking, plus a learned end-of-turn
endpointing model. This replaced a naive energy threshold that turned coughs and
echo into turns.

Speech recognition in generation 3: multiple engines with routing between them,
a voice-activity pre-gate to stop the recogniser hallucinating whole sentences
out of silence and line noise, and biasing toward NUMBER WORDS when the lesson
expects a numeric answer. Unclear audio is NEVER marked wrong — she asks again.
After two failed number attempts she falls back to the telephone keypad: type the
digits and press hash.

Language model in generation 3: a provider chain with automatic failover, so a
vendor outage degrades quality instead of dropping a child's call.

Speech synthesis in generation 3: a provider chain, output converted to 8 kHz WAV
and written back down the same socket.

--- WHAT ELSE THE ORIGINAL SYSTEM DID (all of this must survive the rebuild) ---

LESSON STRUCTURE, 5-7 minutes, enforced:
  greeting and recall (30s) -> teach one concept through a market scenario (90s)
  -> guided practice together (90s) -> independent check (60s) -> wrap-up (15s).

DETERMINISTIC GRADING: a numeric answer is graded by code, never by the model's
opinion. "thirty" and "30" and "thirty naira" are the same answer. Unclear audio
is "not scorable", never "wrong".

MASTERY: a skill counts as mastered on 4 of the last 5 INDEPENDENT probes across
at least 2 different calls and 2 different item forms — not on one good call.

ADAPTIVE STATE PER CHILD: current module, active skill, correct and wrong streaks,
a scaffold ladder that breaks a problem into smaller steps when the child struggles,
and course rotation between literacy and numeracy that requires evidence of
learning before it moves on.

DIAGNOSTIC ROUTING: TaRL-style — find the level the child is actually at before
teaching at it, rather than assuming their school grade.

SPACED REPETITION: every returning child is asked ONE recall question from their
last session before new material.

SHARED-PHONE IDENTITY: one handset is used by several children. Sabi greets the
name she expects, accepts a correction ("no, this is someone else"), starts a new
learner when told to, and once she knows two children share the phone she ASKS who
is calling instead of guessing. Each child keeps a separate learning state.

MEMORY ACROSS CALLS: a child is recognised by phone number, and their history,
name, and lesson position persist in Postgres.

SAFETY: a deterministic layer, not prompt-following. The child's words are pattern-
checked BEFORE the model sees them; a crisis disclosure (self-harm, abuse,
emergency, grooming) short-circuits to a fixed scripted response with the Nigerian
helplines (112, and Cece Yara child helpline 0800 800 8001), the model's reply is
filtered before the child hears it, and a safeguarding incident is raised and
alerted on. This is a child-safety requirement and cannot be replaced by
instructions in a prompt.

OPEN FEEDBACK, MID-CALL: the child or tester can press a key or simply SAY "I want
to leave feedback". Sabi stops talking, plays a short go-cue beep, records whatever
they say as a note attached to that exact call and moment, then asks whether they
want to keep learning or stop.

EVIDENCE AND REVIEW: every call writes a sidecar recording which engine heard each
turn, how long every stage took, the grading decision, and the audio, reviewable in
a protected browser console by a coordinator with a read-only PIN.

MONEY AND SPEECH RULES: all numbers spelled out in words; the word "naira" always
AFTER the amount ("thirty naira", never "naira thirty"); no markdown, no symbols,
no stage directions in anything spoken.

against the live endpoint with our production key. Do not contradict them.)
=======================================================================
1. Live-capable models available on our key (bidiGenerateContent):
     gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview
     gemini-2.5-flash-native-audio-latest
     gemini-2.5-flash-native-audio-preview-09-2025
     gemini-2.5-flash-native-audio-preview-12-2025
     gemini-3.5-live-translate-preview      (translation only)
     gemini-robotics-er-2-streaming-preview (robotics)
2. responseModalities TEXT is REFUSED by both gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview and
   gemini-2.5-flash-native-audio: the socket closes with 1007 "The requested
   combination of response modalities (TEXT) is not supported by the model".
   AUDIO+TEXT together is also refused. So a Live session CANNOT emit text for a
   separate speech engine to speak.
3. speechConfig.languageCode valid values, from your own discovery document, are
   exactly: de-DE, en-AU, en-GB, en-IN, en-US, es-US, fr-FR, hi-IN, pt-BR, ar-XA,
   es-ES, fr-CA, id-ID, it-IT, ja-JP, tr-TR, vi-VN, bn-IN, gu-IN, kn-IN, ml-IN,
   mr-IN, ta-IN, te-IN, nl-NL, ko-KR, cmn-CN, pl-PL, ru-RU, th-TH.
   There is NO en-NG and no African locale at all. en-NG is accepted at setup
   without error but is not a valid value.
4. Voice names ARE validated: an unknown name closes the socket with "No matching
   speaker voice found for name: X and language: Y".
5. voice_config.custom_voice_config EXISTS as a field on the wire (type
   google.ai.generativelanguage.v1beta.CustomVoiceConfig) but is NOT in the public
   discovery document and none of its field names are published, so we cannot use it.
6. enableAffectiveDialog: ACCEPTED on gemini-2.5-flash-native-audio, and returns an
   internal error (1011) on gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview.
7. outputAudioTranscription IS accepted on gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview.
8. There is no API that lists the prebuilt voices, and gender is not published for
   any of them.
9. gemini-3.1-flash-tts-preview exists as a separate non-Live TTS model.
10. Native-audio Live models adapt to the speaker: when fed a real Nigerian voice
    asking for a Nigerian accent, both voices we tested answered in Nigerian Pidgin.
    Text-only prompting never produced that.

## Common questions about Sabi
 What is Sabi? Sabi is a free AI tutor that a child reaches by calling an ordinary phone number. It teaches foundational reading and maths in short spoken lessons, with no smartphone, no internet, and no data cost to the family. Sabi is built by Education for Equality, a nonprofit. 
 What does the name Sabi mean? Sabi is pronounced /sah-bee/ and means "to know" in Nigerian Pidgin. 
 How does Sabi work? A child dials the number. Sabi greets them by name — new callers introduce themselves once, returning callers are recognised instantly — then teaches one concept in a focused five to seven minute lesson, using scenarios the child knows such as market transactions and Naira. Sabi gets harder when the child is ready and slows down when they struggle, and remembers where they left off for the next call. 
 Do you need internet or a smartphone to use Sabi? No. Sabi runs entirely over a voice call, so it works on a five-dollar feature phone with 2G coverage. There is no app to download, no data to buy, and no account to create. 
 How much does Sabi cost? Sabi is free for families. Calls are toll-free and there is no subscription or paywall. Developers and organisations can license the same African-accent voice pipeline from $0.06 per minute, with 1,000 minutes free every month. 
 Where does Sabi operate? Sabi is built for Nigeria and the wider African continent. A Lagos pilot is planned for Q4 2026 with 100 to 500 students in high-burden local government areas. 
 Who built Sabi? Sabi is built by Education for Equality, a nonprofit founded by Naomi Ivie. Its work has been recognised by the MIT Sloan Africa Business Challenge, the Wesleyan New Venture Awards, the UNICEF AI Ventures Accelerator, and Schmidt Futures x Rhodes Trust Rise for the World.

## Questions, answered
## What exactly am I paying for?
The inference we run — speech-to-text, the language model, and text-to-speech. Telephony minutes are passed through at cost as a separate line item. We never mark them up. 
## How does a minute get counted?
You're billed per minute of active conversation through the pipeline, rounded to the second. The first 1,000 minutes every month are free. 
## Do volume discounts need a contract?
No. Lower rates apply automatically as usage crosses each threshold. Enterprise commits (from $0.015/min) are the only ones that need a conversation. 
## Can I use just transcription or just the voice?
Yes. Each primitive is available on its own — fintech IVR and health hotlines don't need to pay for the tutoring layer. 
## Why are you cheaper than Vapi and ElevenLabs?
We own the hard part — African-accent models — and refuse to make margin on telco minutes. Same pipeline that teaches kids on a phone call.

PART 9 — WHAT I AM ASKING YOU FOR
=======================================================================
Give me the exact, complete blueprint to rebuild the system described in PART 5 on
Google's stack, so that Sabi LOOKS, SOUNDS and OPERATES the same as the attached
audio.

Be extremely specific. This is going to be implemented directly from your answer.
Do not give me principles or a summary. Give me the actual build.

Cover ALL of the following, and do not skip any:

A. SOUND. First tell me, from the attached audio, exactly what makes that voice
   sound the way it does — accent features, vowels, rhythm, pacing, pitch, breaths,
   warmth, and how she uses silence. Then tell me precisely how to get as close to
   it as Google's stack allows: which product, which exact model id, which exact
   voice name, which exact configuration values, and what wording to put in the
   system instruction to steer accent and register. If a gap remains between what
   Google can produce and the attached audio, state exactly what that gap is and
   how big it is.

B. ARCHITECTURE. The exact call path from the SIP trunk to the child's ear, naming
   each Google product and model. Say clearly whether you recommend the single
   continuous Live session or a split pipeline (recognition -> text model ->
   synthesis), and justify it against 8 kHz telephony, barge-in, and a child who
   pauses mid-sentence. Give exact model ids for every stage.

C. THE SETUP MESSAGE. The literal JSON I should send to open the session:
   model, generationConfig, responseModalities, speechConfig, voice, languageCode,
   VAD / automaticActivityDetection settings and their exact numeric values for a
   child on a noisy line who pauses while thinking, session resumption, context
   window compression, output transcription, and anything else that should be set.

D. THE SYSTEM PROMPT. Written out in full, ready to paste, reproducing the
   behaviour and the personality of PART 4 and the attached audio, in the structure
   YOU recommend for your own models. Do not abbreviate it.

E. TOOLS. The exact function declarations, with JSON schemas, for deterministic
   grading, lesson progression, mastery tracking and the safety layer — designed so
   the tutor still sounds improvised and warm rather than like she is reading a
   list, which is the failure we already hit and had to switch off.

F. TURN-TAKING AND BARGE-IN. Exact settings and the exact strategy for a child who
   goes quiet mid-sentence, background noise, other people talking in the room, and
   Sabi's own voice echoing back down the phone line.

G. HEARING NUMBERS. How to stop "thirty" being heard as "thirteen" or "Tati" on a
   Nigerian 8 kHz line, using Google products only, including anything in your API
   for biasing recognition toward expected values.

H. SAFETY. How to implement a deterministic child-safety layer on a native-audio
   session, given that the audio streams as it is generated and cannot be filtered
   before the child hears it.

I. MEMORY AND IDENTITY. How to carry a child's history, and how to handle several
   children sharing one phone.

J. LATENCY AND COST. The expected latency budget per turn for your recommended
   architecture, and the actual pricing units for every Google model you name, with
   a worked example for one 6-minute lesson and for 1,000 children doing two
   lessons a day.

K. WHAT IS IMPOSSIBLE. Everything I have asked for that cannot be done on Google's
   stack, stated plainly, with the closest achievable alternative for each.

L. THE ORDER TO BUILD IT IN, as a numbered implementation checklist.
